### 1. When Experts Redraw or Re-Anchor a Trendline

Experts follow specific structural rules to maintain, adjust, or re-anchor sloped trendlines as price action unfolds:

* **The "Lowest Low Preceding Highest High" Anchor Rule (Trader Vic / Sperandeo)**: In classical technical analysis, a standard uptrend line is anchored from the lowest low of the move to the lowest reaction low *preceding the highest high* [1]. If price breaks this initial trendline but subsequently recovers to print a **new higher high**, the trendline is rotated downward and re-anchored to the new intervening reaction low [2]. This fan-like downward rotation reflects a slowing of momentum into a more sustainable, gradual slope rather than a trend death [3].
* **No Price-Cutting Rule (Adam Grimes)**: Standard trendlines are defined as lines connecting successive swing pivots that **must not cut through prices** between attachment points [4, 5]. If price action penetrates or cuts through a line between pivots, or if a new trend high forms after a deeper pullback, the old line is invalidated and a new line must be drawn connecting the valid pivot points [5].
* **Trend Maturation and Flattening (Al Brooks)**: As a trend progresses, steep micro trendlines break frequently [6]. Brooks notes that as counter-trend traders step in, trendlines naturally become **flatter and broader** [7]. Experts adjust their lines from steep micro trendlines to broader channel lines as the market transitions from a strong spike into a wider channel or trading range [7, 8].
* **Mechanical Re-Anchoring (Thomas DeMark / TD Lines)**: DeMark removes subjectivity by anchoring supply and demand lines exclusively to mechanically defined "TD Points" (such as Level 1 or Level 2 pivot highs/lows) [9]. When new qualifying TD Points form, old lines are automatically replaced or updated according to strict bar-count criteria [9].

### 2. When Experts Keep a Line Despite Wick Pokes

Not every penetration invalidates a sloped trendline. Experts decide whether to keep or prune a line based on how price finishes relative to the level:

* **Wicks as Rejection / Zone Buffer**: Price action experts emphasize that trendlines and boundary levels act as **zones rather than zero-thickness lines** [10, 11]. A wick that spears through a line or zone during the bar but recovers to **close back on the correct side** is classified as a test and rejection of the level, confirming that order flow is defending the line rather than breaking it [12, 13].
* **Timeframe Noise and Flag Setups (Al Brooks)**: Intraday wick penetrations through a steep micro trendline on a higher timeframe (e.g., 5-minute chart) often represent minor pullbacks or flag formations on lower timeframes (e.g., 1-minute chart) [6, 14]. In strong trends, wicks poking through a trendline do not require redrawing the main line; instead, they offer high-probability **with-trend entries** (bull/bear flags) [6, 14].
* **Body vs. Wick Construction**: Some experts prefer drawing trendlines through candle bodies (closes) rather than extreme wicks to filter out intrabar noise [8]. However, Grimes warns against "sloppy" candle-cutting unless execution rules strictly operate on bar closes [4].

### 3. Decisive Breakout vs. False Break / Tease

Distinguishing a genuine trendline break from a false breakout relies on specific quantitative and structural filters:

* **Edwards & Magee’s Classical Criteria (Peter Brandt)**: Classical charting applies three strict tests to declare a trendline breakout decisive:
  1. **Extent of Penetration**: Price must close beyond the trendline by a defined margin (typically **3%** for equities/commodities, or **1%–2%** for futures/forex) [15].
  2. **Volume Expansion**: A genuine breakout requires a notable rise in trading volume; breaks on contracting volume are considered "bogus" or suspect [16].
  3. **Underside Roll-Over**: If price feebly reaches the underside of the broken trendline and rolls over, the breakout is confirmed [16].
* **Tease Breaks and Squeezes (Bob Volman)**: A "tease break" occurs when price pierces a boundary or trendline **without prior buildup or consolidation** [17, 18]. Breaks lacking a tight squeeze or doji cluster right under the line frequently trap traders and fail immediately [17, 18]. Real breakouts require a "buildup" where price coils tightly against the level before breaking [19, 20].
* **Failure Metrics (Peter Brandt)**: Brandt notes that **70% of diagonal trendline violations fail** or fizzle out quickly, while only 30% result in a sustained, meaningful move [21, 22].

### 4. Post-Break Significance: Do Broken Sloped Lines Matter?

Once a sloped trendline is broken, its role changes fundamentally depending on whether you look at the diagonal line itself or the horizontal market structure it left behind:

* **Death of the Diagonal Line**: Experts like Adam Grimes state unequivocally that once a sloped trendline is cut or broken, **it is invalidated and should not be projected forward** [5]. Carrying broken diagonal lines into the future clutters charts without providing reliable support or resistance [5]. Furthermore, Peter Brandt emphasizes that violating a trendline carries **no measured price target**; it merely signals that the rate/slope of the prior trend has changed [23, 24].
* **Shift to Horizontal Structure & Role Reversal**: While the sloped line dies, the **horizontal price levels** established at the break point and adjacent swing pivots become primary support and resistance zones [5]. When a major structural level breaks, it undergoes **polarity flip / role reversal** (broken support becomes new resistance, and vice versa) [25-28]. This happens because trapped traders look to exit at breakeven upon a retest, while sidelined traders enter in the direction of the new move [25, 26, 28].
* **Major Trend Reversal Sequence (Al Brooks)**: In price action theory, a trendline break is rarely an immediate full reversal on its own [29]. Instead, the trendline break represents **Step 1** of a major reversal, signaling a transition into a trading range [29, 30]. The market must then retest the old trend's extreme (forming a higher low, lower high, or double top/bottom) before a new opposite trend can be confirmed [29, 31].
