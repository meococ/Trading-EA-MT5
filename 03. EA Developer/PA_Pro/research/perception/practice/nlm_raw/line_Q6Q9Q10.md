### 1. Selection and Economy: How Many Trend Lines at Once?

* **Strict Chart Economy (3–5 Lines Max):** Professional chartists and quantitative researchers emphasize keeping charts stripped down to prevent "chart clutter" and decision paralysis [1-4]. **Peter Brandt** notes that seeing charts covered in lines immediately indicates a failure to apply classical charting principles, remarking that if a chart requires more than 15 seconds of searching for lines, it is time to move on [4, 5]. Similarly, prop firm guidance warns that drawing dozens of lines causes hesitation, which is a primary reason traders blow up funded accounts [2, 3].
* **Layering & Structural Hierarchy:** Rather than drawing equal lines everywhere, professionals structure sloped trendlines hierarchically [6]:
  * **Major / Broad Trendlines:** Define the overall macroeconomic or higher-timeframe trend boundaries that contain all price action [6].
  * **Micro Trendlines:** Drawn across short 2-to-10 bar spans within the broader trend to spot immediate momentum or entry triggers (such as micro-bull or bear flags) [7].
  * **Dueling Trendlines:** At key decision points, traders maintain two intersecting lines—a major long-term trendline and a short-term counter-trendline—to observe how order flow resolves at their point of intersection [8, 9].

---

### 2. Which Line is Operative NOW?

* **The Active Anchor Rule:** According to **Adam Grimes** and **Trader Vic (Victor Sperandeo)**, a valid, operative uptrend line must connect a major pivot low to the lowest low *preceding the highest high* of the current move [10, 11]. As long as price makes new highs without penetrating this line, it remains the active primary trendline [10, 11].
* **Mechanical Pivot Definitions (TD Lines™):** **Tom DeMark** eliminates subjectivity by defining operative lines using **TD Points** [12]. A Level 1 TD Demand Point requires 1 preceding and 1 succeeding higher low (3 bars total), whereas higher-magnitude TD Points (Level 2 to Level 9) require more surrounding bars [12]. The most recent, qualified TD Points form the active TD Supply or Demand lines [12].
* **Immediate Invalidation & Pruning:** The moment price decisively breaks or cuts through a trendline, the line is invalidated and **must be pruned from the chart** [11, 13, 14]. Carrying stale or broken trendlines forward is considered a major analytical mistake [11, 14].

---

### 3. Choosing Among Valid Lines: Newest vs. Most-Touched vs. Steepest

* **Steepest vs. Flatter (Trend Maturity):** **Al Brooks** explains that steep trendlines (such as steep micro-trendlines) reflect initial, aggressive momentum where traders should only take with-trend positions [7]. However, as a trend matures, steep lines naturally break and trendlines flatten out [15]. Flatter, broader trendlines represent the true macro support/resistance as deep pullbacks develop [15].
* **Most-Touched vs. Freshness (The Order Thinning Paradox):**
  * **Validation:** Classical TA states that 2 points form a trendline, but a **3rd touchpoint confirms its validity** [16, 17].
  * **Order Thinning:** Sources caution that while multiple touches confirm a level's visibility, testing a line 3 or 4+ times thins out the resting orders defending it [18, 19]. A 4th or 5th touch is much more likely to break than a 2nd or 3rd touch because the order pool has been chewed through [18, 19].
* **Newest / Dynamic Adjustments:** In real-time trading, trendlines are dynamic [20]. Whenever the market prints a new higher high, the trendline must be redrawn/adjusted from the original low to the new lowest low preceding that new high [10, 20].

---

### 4. What Pros Deliberately DO NOT Draw & Common Beginner Mistakes

#### What Pros Deliberately DO NOT Draw:
* **Lines Cutting Through Price Bars/Wicks:** **Adam Grimes** and **Trader Vic** explicitly instruct: *never draw trendlines through price bars or wicks* [21-23]. Slicing through price to force a line to fit later data is considered sloppy and invalidates the line's predictive value [23, 24].
* **Lines in Flat Markets (No Swings):** Trendlines define relationships between distinct swing pivots; drawing diagonal lines across flat, sideways markets without clear swings produces meaningless lines that get sliced constantly [24, 25].
* **Nonstandard Lines:** Pros do not draw lines connecting lows in a downtrend (instead of lower highs) or connecting highs in an uptrend (instead of higher lows) [24].
* **Broken / Stale Lines:** Keeping lines on the chart long after price has sliced through them [11, 14].

#### Common Beginner Mistakes:
* **Over-Drawing / Chart Clutter:** Filling the chart with dozens of lines until every 10-point move hits a line, creating complete execution paralysis [2-4].
* **Fighting Steep Momentum:** Taking counter-trend trades when a steep micro-trendline is intact, rather than waiting for a clear trendline break and retest [7, 15].
* **Treating Lines as Exact 1-Pixel Prices:** Expecting price to bounce off a diagonal line to the exact tick rather than treating support/resistance as a flexible zone or band, causing premature stop-outs [2, 19, 26].
* **Assuming a Line Break Equals a Full Reversal:** Treating a trendline penetration as an automatic trend reversal with a measured move target, when in reality a break usually only signals a **change in trend slope/pace** or consolidation [27, 28].

---

### 5. Where the Experts DISAGREE

* **Reliability of Sloped/Diagonal Lines vs. Horizontal Zones:**
  * **Peter Brandt** expresses deep skepticism toward diagonal constructions (trendlines, triangles, wedges), noting that in his 40+ years of trading, diagonal breakouts have only a **30% win rate** (with 70% failing or fizzling out), compared to horizontal patterns [29-31]. He famously quips that giving a ruler to an ape will eventually yield a diagonal line that seems to make sense [32].
  * **Futures / Order-Flow Traders** view trendlines as the weakest form of support/resistance due to extreme subjectivity, using them strictly for general direction context rather than trade entries [33].
  * **Al Brooks, Adam Grimes, and Tom DeMark**, in contrast, view sloped trendlines as indispensable, foundational tools for reading market structure, entering pullbacks, and projecting targets [12, 34, 35].
* **Subjective Visual Pivots vs. Rigid Mechanical Rules:**
  * **Tom DeMark (TD Lines™)** rejects subjective drawing entirely, enforcing strict mathematical bar counts (TD Points) and mechanical qualification tests for breakouts [12].
  * **Classical Technicians (Brandt, Grimes, Vic)** rely on visual swing identification and higher-timeframe pattern context (e.g., Brandt requiring 12+ weeks of pattern development for high-conviction trades) [10, 11, 36].
* **Wicks vs. Candle Bodies:**
  * **Grimes & Trader Vic** demand drawing strictly against the extreme price points (wicks) without cutting any price action [22, 23].
  * **Al Brooks & S/R Guides** note that in fast or noisy markets, drawing trendlines using candle bodies rather than wicks often provides a cleaner representation of core market structure [6].
