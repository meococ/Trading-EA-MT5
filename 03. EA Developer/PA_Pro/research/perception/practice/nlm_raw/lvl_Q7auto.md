### Level Rules Mechanical Enough to Automate Causally

Several level-identification and zone-construction methodologies within the corpus are governed by strict, deterministic rules that can be fully coded into automated algorithms:

*   **Mechanical Pivot Detection & Trendline Construction**:
    *   **Tom DeMark's TD Lines™**: DeMark defines TD Points through exact bar-count conditions [1]. A Level 1 TD Point high/low requires a bar's high/low to be immediately preceded and succeeded by bars with lower highs or higher lows (a 3-bar rule) [1]. Higher-magnitude TD Points (Level 2 up to Level 9) scale the exact number of required preceding and succeeding bars, allowing for fully automated, objective trendline construction, breakout qualification, and price projections [1].
    *   **Fixed-Lookback Market Structure**: Automated toolkits (such as LuxAlgo's Price Action Concepts) construct swing points (HH, HL, LH, LL) and market structure signals (Change of Character / CHoCH and Break of Structure / BOS) by applying fixed lookback windows (e.g., 5–49 bars for internal structure, 50–100 bars for swing structure) [2, 3].
    *   **Kernel Regression & Nonparametric Extrema**: Academic frameworks (such as Lo, Mamaysky & Wang, 2000) automate pattern recognition by applying kernel regression to rolling price windows (e.g., 38 trading days) [4-6]. Local extrema are detected mechanically wherever the derivative of the smoothed price curve changes sign (\\(\text{Sgn}(\hat{m}'_h(t)) \neq \text{Sgn}(\hat{m}'_h(t+1))\\)), identifying geometric patterns (double tops/bottoms, head & shoulders, rectangles) without visual bias [5, 7, 8]. Heuristic discovery algorithms similarly map and evaluate intraday support/resistance levels statistically based on bounce frequencies and touch counts [9].
*   **Zone Boundary & Width Construction Rules**:
    *   **Deterministic Boundary Methods**: ChartMini lays out four mathematical ways to set horizontal support/resistance zone boundaries from a set of price reactions [107, 120, 403–406]:
        1.  *Close-Cluster Boundaries*: Upper and lower edges set strictly to the highest and lowest qualifying completed bar closes [10, 11].
        2.  *Body-Range Boundaries*: Upper and lower edges set to the open-to-close body extremes of selected candles [10, 12].
        3.  *Wick-Envelope Boundaries*: Boundaries set from the lowest qualifying wick low to the highest qualifying wick high [10, 13].
        4.  *Core plus Wick Envelope*: A two-layer area maintaining a tight core (closes/bodies) surrounded by an outer envelope (wick extremes) [10, 14].
    *   **Algorithmic Width & Maintenance Rules**: Zone width is defined strictly as \\(\text{Upper Boundary} - \text{Lower Boundary}\\) (measured in pips, ticks, or percentage of midpoint) [15, 16]. Updating or retiring zones follows written rules (e.g., issuing a versioned update \\(v_1 \to v_2\\) when a new qualifying close occurs outside the boundary; retiring levels when cleanly broken by volume) [88, 108, 119, 415–418].
*   **Fixed Reference Anchors**:
    *   **Session & Psychological Levels**: Mechanically plotting prior regular trading hours (RTH) highs/lows, overnight session extremes, prior day closes, 50% retracement levels, or psychological round numbers (ending in 00 or 50) provides objective, unambiguous levels [17-23].
    *   **Order-Flow & Volume Anchors**: Combining horizontal price levels with Volume Profile High-Volume Nodes (HVNs), Value Area boundaries, session VWAP, or initializing Wyckoff trading range boundaries (setting resistance at the Buying Climax peak and support at the Automatic Reaction low) can be executed algorithmically upon event trigger [17, 24-27].

### Parts That Still Require Human Judgment

Despite the availability of mechanical rules, the corpus highlights several critical areas where fully automated systems struggle and human judgment remains necessary:

*   **Location Context & Market Regime Filtering**:
    *   **Location as a Filter**: A candlestick rejection pattern (pin bar, hammer, engulfing bar) carries no inherent predictive value in open space or mid-range; its validity depends entirely on forming at a pre-identified higher-timeframe (HTF) zone [28-32].
    *   **Trend Regimes vs. Level Validity**: Automated systems frequently fail in strong trend environments because powerful moves easily slice through support/resistance levels ("strong trends eat levels") [33]. Judging whether a market is trending, ranging, or undergoing a major structural reversal requires contextual analysis [33, 34]. Furthermore, scheduled economic news releases override technical chart structure, requiring macro calendar oversight [33].
*   **Subjective Geometry & Level Selection**:
    *   **Pattern Completion & Vertical Isolation**: Classical chartists (such as Peter Brandt) emphasize that chart patterns must be vertically isolated and completed before trading; drawing diagonal trendlines or identifying complex formations is highly subjective and prone to misinterpretation [35-37]. Adam Grimes notes that trendlines are "the most used and abused tool" because traders draw them subjectively, making objective evaluation difficult [38].
    *   **Grading & Pruning Levels**: Distinguishing between "A-grade" (strong HTF levels with multiple sharp reactions) and "Weak" levels, determining whether a break is clean or merely an overshoot/liquidity sweep, and avoiding chart clutter (keeping only 3–5 key levels) require human discretion [19, 20, 39-41].
    *   **Algorithmic Approximation Limits**: Academic researchers (Lo, Mamaysky & Wang) explicitly state that pattern recognition algorithms only approximate human chart reading and "may miss some of the more subtle nuances that human cognition is capable of discerning" [42]. Furthermore, standard automated cross-validation for bandwidth selection in kernel regression yielded curves that professional chartists judged as overly smooth, requiring human trial-and-error tuning [43, 44].
*   **Order Flow & Participant Intent Interpretation**:
    *   **Reading Absorption vs. Distribution**: Differentiating Wyckoff Reaccumulation (institutional supply absorption) from Distribution cannot be accomplished by raw OHLC geometry alone; it requires evaluating volume asymmetry, bar spread narrowing, and dampening volatility versus expanding volatility [45-49]. OHLC candles alone cannot reveal hidden limit order quantities or order exhaustion [46, 47].
    *   **Psychological Discretion**: Support and resistance reflect human cognitive biases (such as anchoring bias and breakeven exit behavior) [50-52]. Discretionary traders (such as Lance Beggs) emphasize knowing when to step back and stand aside when market price flow becomes "choppy" or unreadable—a form of self-awareness that automated code lacks [53].
