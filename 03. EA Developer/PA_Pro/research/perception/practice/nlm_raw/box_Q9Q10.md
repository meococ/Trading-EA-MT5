### What Professionals Deliberately Do NOT Draw

* **Chart Clutter & Minor Swing Lines:** Professionals avoid marking every minor swing, candle body, or 10-point intraday oscillation [1-3]. Peter Brandt enforces a strict rule that drawing **more than four lines on a 12-month chart is too many** [4, 5], while futures prop-traders limit themselves to **3–5 key horizontal levels** per instrument [6-8]. Lance Beggs and Brandt both note that legitimate patterns "pop off the page"—if you spend more than 15 seconds searching for a level, it is time to turn the page [9].
* **Diagonal Boundaries on Ranges:** Classical chartists like Peter Brandt deliberately avoid drawing diagonal trendlines, wedges, or symmetrical triangles when defining range consolidations, relying exclusively on **horizontal boundaries** [5, 10, 11].
* **Lines Cutting Through Price:** Trendline and range guidelines explicitly mandate that lines should **never cut through candle bodies or price bars** [12-14].
* **Rigid Single-Pixel Lines for Area Bounces:** Experts deliberately avoid drawing single razor-thin lines across area bounces, preferring **shaded boxes or two-line zones** to account for market noise and overshoot [1, 15-18].
* **Reactive or Hindsight Lines:** Professionals do not draw or move lines in real time after a candle forms (hindsight bias); levels are plotted **before the session opens** [19-21].
* **Stale or Broken Levels:** Once price cleanly breaks through a boundary with volume and strong closes, the level is immediately **deleted rather than left on the chart** [15, 22, 23].

### Common Beginner Mistakes with Ranges

* **Over-Drawing & "Blueprint" Charts:** Beginners frequently mark every minor pivot, creating a cluttered "blueprint" chart that leads to decision paralysis and manufactured signals [3, 6, 8, 24-26].
* **Treating Range Boundaries as Exact Prices:** Beginners expect price to respect a level to the exact pip or tick, leading to premature stop-outs when price slightly overshoots or wicks into a zone [3, 16-19].
* **Hindsight Level Shifting:** Novice traders often expand or adjust their range boxes after a candle forms to make past setups look successful [19-21].
* **Ignoring Higher-Timeframe Trend Context:** Beginners attempt to trade counter-trend range bounces inside powerful trending environments where supply or demand will easily steamroll horizontal support/resistance [19, 23, 27].
* **Forcing Interpretations:** Novices spend endless time staring at ambiguous charts trying to "force" a range structure where none exists [9, 28].
* **Trading Without Buildup or Confirmation:** Beginners jump into range boundaries on first contact without waiting for rejection candles, buildup, or volume confirmation, exposing themselves to "tease breaks" and false breakouts [20, 29-32].
* **Anchor Bias on Stale Levels:** Keeping broken or weeks-old levels on the chart long after they have lost relevance [15, 23, 32].

### Key Points of Disagreement Among Corpus Experts

1. **Single Horizontal Line vs. Two-Line Box/Zone**
   * **Zone / Box Advocates** (*Candlestick Patterns Guide, PropTradingVibes, ChartMini*): Insist that **"zones beat lines"** because price rarely respects a 1-pixel line, making shaded 5–10 point boxes necessary to accommodate wicks and spread widening [15-18, 22].
   * **Line Advocates & Hybrids** (*Phil / Antivestor, Rayner Teo*): Phil advocates switching to a **line-on-close chart** to define exact horizontal line levels [33]. Rayner Teo uses 1 line when price respects a level to the pip and 2 lines when price bounces off an area, noting that he often uses single lines to keep charts clean while treating them as zones conceptually [1, 34].

2. **Candle Closes vs. Extreme Wicks for Range Edges**
   * **Close / Body Method** (*PropTradingVibes, ChartMini*): Uses **candle body closes as the primary reference** for range boundaries because closes represent where price actually settled, treating wicks as temporary probes [22, 35, 36].
   * **Wick-Envelope Method** (*Candlestick Patterns Guide, ChartMini*): Sets range boundaries spanning from the **extreme wick to the cluster of closes** to capture the full price range [16, 17, 37].
   * **Strict Non-Cutting** (*Trendlines 101*): Rejects drawing lines through candle wicks or shadows, calling it sloppy practice [14].

3. **Horizontal-Only vs. Diagonal/Dynamic Boundaries**
   * **Horizontal Exclusivity** (*Peter Brandt*): Rejects diagonal trendlines and angled patterns as the **most unreliable constructions**, insisting on horizontal area boundaries [5, 10, 11].
   * **Dynamic & Diagonal Inclusion** (*Al Brooks, Bruce Fraser / Wyckoff, Lance Beggs, Rayner Teo*): Routinely draw **diagonal trendlines, trend channels, and moving averages** alongside horizontal boundaries to define range and market structure [38-44].

4. **Impact of Multiple Touches (Level Strength vs. Order Exhaustion)**
   * **Classical Strength View** (*Phil / Antivestor, Rayner Teo*): More touchpoints (**3+ touches**) make a horizontal range level stronger, more tradable, and more reliable [42, 45, 46].
   * **Order Exhaustion View** (*Candlestick Patterns Guide, ChartMini*): Each subsequent test **chews through resting limit orders**, meaning a 4th or 5th touch actually *weakens* a level and increases breakout probability [47-49].

5. **Minimum Touch Thresholds**
   * **Three Touch Minimum** (*Phil / Antivestor*): Requires at least **3 touchpoints** before considering a horizontal level tradable [45, 46].
   * **Two Touch Minimum** (*Candlestick Patterns Guide, PropTradingVibes*): **Two clear reactions** establish a workable level, whereas one touch is merely a guess [2, 15, 17, 50].
